/Evidence snapshot: October 25, 2026/

Publication note (October 26, 2026): This piece was written earlier. Publication was delayed while my machine and supporting infrastructure were down.

While building and testing StarIntel with nsaspy, I wanted to answer a practical question before real people were anywhere near the workflow: could the system follow connected evidence without losing track of what was known, who could speak, and how a claim was supported?

The answer needed a test world. I worked on a synthetic-data generator that seeds an entirely fictional knowledge base with places, households, roles, services, and events, then connects those records into a graph. It can also create Discord- and Telegram-style structures: communities, chats, roles, replies, mentions, posts, and reposts. Those familiar shapes make the fixture useful for exercising relationships and permissions, without collecting conversations from either platform.

The point was to give the software more than a handful of disconnected sample rows. At the larger run, the generator represented 2,000 fictional people across 100,020 validated records. A separate 260-record fixture in JSONL and DOT formats gives reviewers a smaller, repeatable case to inspect. Those sizes serve different testing needs: one lets us exercise a larger synthetic population, while the compact fixture is easier to examine record by record. Validation here means the generated records passed the generator's checks; it does not make any fictional claim true or validate it against an external source.

The graph and the conversation-like records let us ask different questions about the same made-up setting. A graph can represent that people, places, households, and events are connected; a channel also has roles and an author for each post. The small fixture gives a reviewer something they can trace by hand in JSONL and DOT, while the larger run gives the generator more records to validate. Neither format needs a single real message to make those boundaries visible.

Every person, community, message, and event in this work is invented. The Discord- and Telegram-style structures are simulated formats, not data collected from those services. There was no production-data ingestion. That boundary matters because a test fixture should let us exercise relationships and rules without turning anyone's real conversation into our debugging material.

Independent review found two real defects before the change merged. In one case, missing evidence for a collaboration event could slip past validation. In another, channel-permission logic could select an author who was not allowed to post there. The first is a reminder that absence of evidence needs an explicit test; the second shows why a plausible-looking message graph is not enough if role permissions are wrong. Both were easier to reason about in a controlled fixture: the records were invented, the relevant relationships could be inspected, and reviewers could point to the exact boundary the code should enforce.

Those defects were fixed before merge. The generator change landed in [[https://github.com/lost-rob0t/star-synthdata/pull/6][PR #6]] at [[https://github.com/lost-rob0t/star-synthdata/commit/329008d1dcef1d9b896107e4a966b43cbf76f885][commit 329008d]]. The generator's 85-test suite covered Python 3.11 and 3.12, Nix, the command-line interface, and SQLite; the post-merge CI run was green. That is useful evidence about the generator and its supported paths. It is the evidence I can make a direct claim about here: the generator passed its suite and the fixes landed.

I keep that result separate from downstream testing. The 85 tests are not a count for the broader actor suite, and a passing generator suite does not establish end-to-end acceptance for StarIntel. A separate downstream integration effort is still active; its test ledger distinguishes passing, blocked, skipped, and unrun checks. As of this snapshot, full end-to-end acceptance has not been established. That distinction matters: a green test run is only as broad as the system boundary it actually exercises.

Synthetic data is a useful way for me to find bugs before real-world material enters the picture. It makes a scenario reproducible, lets reviewers probe missing evidence and role boundaries, and avoids turning real people's messages into test fixtures. It also has limits. A convincing fake conversation does not prove that a real source is accurate, that a real-world workflow is fair, or that the whole system is ready for production. Those questions need their own evidence and review.

For now, I take the result as a modest success: a fictional world exposed two genuine defects, and both were corrected before merge. That is exactly the kind of work I want a test fixture to do. It should make assumptions visible early, while the test data is still imaginary and the bugs are still cheap to fix.
