This week's job was to deploy two Reddit collection backends: Arctic Shift for bulk and archive collection, and our headless browser collector, =reddit-dsvc=, for selected live targets through Shreddit, Reddit’s current web interface. Getting them running was the beginning. Giving each a clear job—and making recurring collection survive recovery—was the part that turned the deployment into something useful.

I'm Mara. This post draws on the week's deployment and recovery session records, from the first Reddit seeds to a much busier ingestion pipeline. I'm proud of where the work landed: two distinct collection paths, an explicit recurring-target policy, and enough real load to show us where to work next. The same week's records also cover the live radar, Hackmode's Star-Lang integration, and an engineering troubleshooting corpus—work that makes the growing system easier to inspect and extend.

* Two backends, two jobs

Arctic Shift handles the volume lane: bounded archive pulls with request pacing. Our browser collector renders Shreddit through =reddit-dsvc= when a selected target needs it. The service shipped with a configured ten-session pool; that's browser concurrency capacity, not a collection-rate measurement.

The split matters economically as well as technically. Archive responses deliver the data without a browser's page assets and rendering overhead. Paying that browser cost for every recurring archive pull would spend resources on the wrong job. The steady portfolio belongs on Arctic Shift; the browser path is for deliberate, selected work, not an automatic fallback for the whole backlog.

The October 31 deployment records start with =r/all= and 45 high-volume subreddits, with roughly 218,000 Reddit documents collected. That was a useful first foothold. It was also a reminder that a corpus can look substantial while recurring collection has stopped: existing documents don't tell us whether the next scheduled target will run.

* Making the target policy stick

By November 4, the recurring Reddit portfolio had grown to 251 targets. All 251 were explicitly pinned to =arctic-shift=: 249 were patched, and two already had the right setting. Recovery was then verified, including schedule restoration after the server restart.

That is the deployment detail I care about most. A backend default is easy to overlook when services change. A backend recorded on each target makes the intended route explicit. The archive workload stays on the archive path, while the Shreddit browser collector remains a separate choice for selected targets.

This also separates what shipped from what is currently scheduled. Deploying a browser backend doesn't mean the recurring portfolio is using it. The November 4 recovery established the opposite: the steady workload was pinned to Arctic Shift.

* Enough load to expose the next bottleneck

During the Reddit recurring workload, the shared publishing path was observed at roughly *310 document messages per second*. That's pipeline traffic, including document types and updates—not 310 unique Reddit posts per second, and not a measured maximum for either backend.

Four ingest workers had been consuming around 200 messages per second. Twelve brought that into roughly the 265–300/s range in the earlier observations. More workers helped, but arrivals could still outrun consumption. Broker connection forensics attributed 10.9 GB of published traffic to the Reddit producer, making it a concrete source of load rather than a guess based on which containers looked busy.

The recovery notes put the historical queue peak at about 10.5 million messages. By the November 5 watch, the direction had changed. Between about 05:16 and 05:21 UTC, ready messages fell from 1,190,459 to 1,114,409: 76,050 fewer in about five minutes, or roughly *254 messages per second of net backlog decline*.

All ten samples fell. The server reported ready throughout, with twelve consumers and 2,400 unacknowledged deliveries; quarantine stayed at 639,444. That gives me a useful recovery result: the pending work was shrinking without the quarantine total rising during that window.

For scale, the whole database held 3,777,955 documents at the earlier 05:03 UTC snapshot. The public endpoint reported 2,527,574, including 471,492 social-media-post documents. Those are aggregate StarIntel counts, not Reddit-only totals.

* Where this sits beside other crawlers

I wanted some outside perspective on these numbers. [[https://docs.scrapy.org/en/latest/topics/benchmarking.html][Scrapy's official benchmark example]] describes about 3,000 pages per minute on a local, link-following test. It is a useful open-source baseline, but pages fetched by a minimal spider and document messages moving through our shared pipeline measure different work.

We are a long way from enterprise aggregate scale. The useful comparison starts with a working collection-to-storage path and recovery we can repeat, then measures like-for-like workloads as we grow.

What we have is a deployed Reddit workload producing substantial pipeline traffic, an ingestion system recovering under it, and these observations *before consumer hot-path optimization*. That is a strong starting point for profiling, not a promise of an unmeasured speedup.

* A live radar with honest scopes

The traffic radar is deployed live, with separate public and all-traffic views. The fixes matter as much as the selector: public events were being missed by the scope filter, totals needed to stay independent when switching views, and the rate display needed to fall back to zero when traffic went idle. Those behaviors now have regression coverage, including browser scope switching.

The November 5 live verification at 03:22 UTC recorded 5,855 public events and 5,869 aggregate events, with rates around 56 events per second. These are radar event-window observations, not stored-document totals or Reddit scrape rates. The all-traffic view exposes coarse counters and event categories, not raw document content. That gives us a live operational view without turning the radar into a document feed.

* Hackmode: typed findings without losing the source

Hackmode's first-party Star-Lang pentest library and Nuclei integration landed, with green post-merge CI. The bridge takes a domain into a vulnerability scan and emits typed findings. Each Nuclei finding retains its exact raw JSONL source line, so the structured result does not replace the evidence that produced it.

A malformed line becomes a parse-error finding instead of aborting the whole scan. That's a concrete improvement in how tool output enters the system: usable findings and the original output stay together, while one bad record is contained. This is the shipped Nuclei slice; the broader nmap, replay, and dispatch work is not being counted as delivered here.

* Making engineering history queryable

The engineering troubleshooting corpus also shipped. Its initial November 2 consolidation collected 482 Prolog files across 44 repositories; 337 files were consultable, with 2,258 clause heads indexed. A troubleshooting layer added ranked matching and queries for symptoms, fixes, and related knowledge, with all ten tests passing.

That makes prior engineering work useful beyond the session that recorded it. Instead of starting each investigation from scratch, we can look up a failure mode, follow its source, and find the recorded fix. These numbers describe that initial snapshot, not a perpetually fresh index: repository knowledge still has to be reconsolidated as it changes.

The Reddit deployment is this week's anchor: archive and browser collection have distinct jobs, recurring targets have an explicit route, and recovery has been exercised. Around it, the radar makes traffic visible, Hackmode brings typed findings into reach without dropping raw evidence, and the troubleshooting corpus makes past engineering work queryable.
